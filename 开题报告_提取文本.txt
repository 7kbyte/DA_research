Forgetting and Retention in Language Model
Domain Adaptation under a Fixed Replay Budget
Liuqing Wang 2023010762 online_wlq@foxmail.com
October 8, 2026
Abstract
Domain adaptation can improve a language model on new text while degrading its performance
on earlier distributions. This project will investigate how replay sources, replay objectives, and
sample selection affect this trade-off under a fixed replay budget. A controlled study will com-
pare substitute general-domain text and self-generated text, cross-entropy and teacher–student
KL constraints, and random, stratified, and cluster-based selection. Experiments will use Pythia-
410M, mathematical domain text, and independent general-domain evaluation sets. Unique replay
coverage, cumulative replay exposure, and actual computation will be recorded separately. The
intended contribution is a reproducible comparison and an analysis of when lightweight selection
helps or fails. Main experiments are planned for eight weeks, followed by thesis preparation. No
training results are claimed in this proposal.
1 Background and Related Work
Continued training on a specialized corpus is a practical way to adapt a pretrained language model.
However, updates that fit the new distribution may interfere with capabilities supported by its existing
parameters. This resembles catastrophic forgetting in computer vision continual learning: both face a
stability–plasticity trade-off and employ parameter constraints, output distillation, or replay. Elastic
weight consolidation, Learning without Forgetting, and deep generative replay provide foundational
examples [ 5, 6, 7]. Here, domain adaptation means continued next-token prediction training, and
forgetting means unintended performance degradation, distinct from deliberate machine unlearning.
Recent language-model studies examine different aspects of this problem. Chang et al. [ 1] inject
fictional facts during pretraining and track acquisition and subsequent decay using answer-span log
probabilities. Such probes measure controlled knowledge retention; they are not equivalent to free-
generation accuracy. Bethune et al. [ 2] study pretraining-data injection during fine-tuning. Small
injection rates, around 1% in their settings, substantially mitigate forgetting, while repeatedly using
a small replay bank can cause overfitting. These rates should not be assumed optimal for a different
model or domain. Marek et al. [ 3] show that self-generated replay can substitute for original data
in some settings, with KL constraints often more stable than cross-entropy, but outcomes depend on
capacity and optimization.
Replay selection is already an established idea. Self-Synthesized Rehearsal (SSR) combines in-
struction synthesis, output refinement, and representative selection through clustering [ 8]. Kotha
and Liang [ 9] show that replay can also improve target performance, so its effect cannot be de-
scribed solely as sacrificing adaptation for retention. Wu et al. [ 4] explain benefits of data mixtures
under specific high-dimensional regression assumptions; this does not guarantee retention in lan-
guage models. Broader surveys and empirical continual fine-tuning studies further motivate explicit
evaluation choices [10, 11].

2 Research Questions and Scope
The central question is: How do replay source, objective, and sample selection influence domain
adaptation and retention when replay resources are limited?
1. Source and objective: How do substitute general-domain and self-generated replay compare
under cross-entropy (CE) and a frozen-teacher KL constraint?
2. Selection: At equal replay-bank size, do teacher-NLL stratification or cluster representatives
improve the retention–adaptation trade-off relative to random selection?
3. Budget and cost: How sensitive are results to cumulative replay exposure, and do any benefits
justify generation, teacher inference, and selection costs?
The contribution will be a controlled empirical comparison, rather than a claim that generated replay,
KL distillation, or clustering is novel. A plausible hypothesis is that selection reduces finite-bank
variability; another is that KL improves retention by preserving the teacher’s soft predictions. Both
may fail when generated text poorly covers evaluation distributions. The required scope is one main
model, one target domain, and three seeds. Full pretraining, broad scaling-law fitting, and a new
general-purpose continual-learning framework are excluded.
3 Methodology
3.1 Model and Data
The main model will be the final checkpoint of Pythia-410M [ 12], with a 160M model available for
debugging. Full-parameter continued training will use English mathematical text from FineMath’s
finemath-4plus subset. C4 English will supply substitute general-domain replay. Held-out C4
documents and WikiText-103 will provide separate general-domain evaluations. C4 is not claimed
to be Pythia’s actual pretraining corpus, and mathematical text likelihood is not a direct measure of
mathematical reasoning.
Public model and dataset cards have been checked. FineMath provides only a training split, so
document-level training, validation, and test partitions must be constructed before token chunking.
General-domain evaluation documents will be disjoint from replay, with cross-split duplicate checks.
V ersions, document identifiers, tokenization, and subset construction will be recorded. Budgets will
be measured using the model’s tokenizer rather than dataset-card token counts.

4 Evaluation, Budgets, and Feasibility
4.1 Retention and Adaptation
For the adapted checkpoint θft, the primary quantities are
Fold = Lold(θft) − Lold(θ0), G new = Lnew(θ0) − Lnew(θft). (2)
Larger Fold indicates greater degradation on a specified old distribution; larger Gnew indicates better
target adaptation. Results for C4 and WikiText-103 will be reported separately. Teacher–student
KL on held-out data will serve as an output-drift diagnostic, not a replacement for performance
evaluation.
Methods will be compared at equal target-token exposure and, where feasible, within a common
achieved target-validation-loss range. This helps distinguish effective retention from simply learning
the new domain less. Checkpoints and hyperparameters will be selected using validation data; final
results will use independent tests. Three seeds will yield means, standard deviations, individual runs,
and retention–adaptation trajectories. Conclusions will be limited to the evaluated distributions,
without claiming retention of all pretrained knowledge.
4.2 Resource Accounting and Risk Control
Unique replay-bank size K will be distinguished from cumulative replay ratio b = TR/Tnew, where
TR and Tnew count processed replay and target tokens. The fraction of replay among all processed
tokens is p = b/(1+ b). Initial settings are approximately 2M unique target tokens, 10M target-token
exposures, a 1M-token generated candidate pool, a 0.1M-token replay bank, and b = 0 .05. Pilots
will check feasibility and then freeze these settings before formal comparisons.
Eight groups with three seeds give 24 main runs. Additional b = 0 .01 and 0.10 runs for B4 and M
add 12, for 36 planned formal runs. Pilot and tuning runs will be counted separately. A small shared
learning-rate and λ search will be specified in advance, with equal tuning allowances for comparable
methods. Target training schedules and preprocessing will be held fixed where applicable.
Four RTX 5090 GPUs are available for independent runs; throughput and memory use will be mea-
sured rather than inferred from GPU count. Costs will include generation, teacher inference, feature
extraction, selection, training, and evaluation. Equal token budgets will not be described as equal
compute. The five core baselines take priority if the schedule becomes tight. Main risks are weak
generated coverage, mismatch between substitute replay and old evaluations, or no gain from se-
lection. Negative results will still support useful ablations and failure-condition analysis. A second
domain, a roughly 1B model, or LoRA [ 13] is optional and outside the required scope.

5 Schedule and Expected Outcomes
The main study is planned for six to eight weeks within a three-month thesis schedule. The one-day
proposal deadline covers literature synthesis and planning, not completed experiments.
Period Milestone
Weeks 1–2 Complete core reading; fix splits; validate evaluation and short runs; measure costs.
Weeks 3–4 Run source–objective baselines; plot trajectories; draft methods.
Weeks 5–6 Evaluate selection, deduplication, and replay-budget sensitivity.
Weeks 7–8 Complete three seeds; finalize figures and freeze main experiments.
Weeks 9–12 Write and revise the thesis; prepare defense; add only necessary checks.
Deliverables will include reproducible code and configurations, split and budget records, retention–
adaptation plots, a cost comparison, and the undergraduate thesis. Six core or closely related papers
and public resource cards have been collected; model weights and training subsets have not yet been
validated through training. The goal is a defensible answer to the research questions, with no prior
promise of improvement or publication.
